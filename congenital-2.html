● Source Content Craniosynostosis — Definition

It is the premature fusion of one or more cranial sutures separating the membranous bones of the skull. One or more fibrous sutures in an infant skull prematurely fuse by ossification, changing the growth pattern of the skull. Because the skull cannot expand perpendicular to the fused suture, it compensates by growing more in the direction parallel to the closed sutures.

● Source Content Craniosynostosis — Types
  1. Metopic synostosis: ridging of the metopic suture with bilateral flattening of the frontal bones and anterior displacement of the coronal suture → Trigonocephaly.
  2. Sagittal synostosis (most common): ridging of the fused sagittal suture with frontal & occipital bossing and flattening of parietal bones; elongated antero-posterior head → Scaphocephaly.
  3. Unicoronal synostosis: ridging of half of the coronal suture with flattening of the ipsilateral frontal and parietal bones; orbit deformity from elevation of the sphenoid ridge, commonly described as harlequin eye → Anterior Plagiocephaly.
  4. Bicoronal synostosis: ridging of the coronal suture with flattening of the caudal frontal bones and supraorbital ridges; skull broad and short with lateral expansion and bilateral harlequin orbital configuration → Brachycephaly. It may be part of congenital craniofacial syndromes Apert syndrome or Crouzon syndrome.
  5. Lambdoid synostosis: ridging of the fused lambdoid suture with flattening of the ipsilateral parieto-occipital region → Posterior Plagiocephaly.
  6. Pansynostosis (Craniostenosis): skull assumes a towering shape → Oxycephaly, with marked increase of intracranial tension.
● Source Content Craniosynostosis — Clinical Manifestations
  • Cosmetic disfigurement
  • Neurological manifestations — more common when two or more sutures undergo premature fusion:
    1. Restrained brain growth → may result in mental retardation, motor impairment, and optic atrophy.
    2. Increased intracranial pressure — mostly in pansynostosis.
● Source Content Craniosynostosis — Investigations
  1. Plain skull radiography: shows suture fusion and associated cranial/orbital deformity if present.
  2. CT brain with 3-D skull reconstruction: shows suture fusion with the thumbprint appearance.
  3. CT/MRI brain: required in patients with coronal, metopic, or lambdoid synostosis; may show associated intracranial anomalies.

Figure 8 in the source demonstrates copper beaten appearance on plain skull X-ray.

● Source Content Craniosynostosis — Treatment
  • Suturectomy — reopening of the prematurely closed suture.
  • Cranial remodeling.
  • Facial advancement in cases of proptosis.
● Source Content Myelomeningocele — Definition

This is the most common form of open spinal dysraphism (spina bifida). It is characterized by herniation of dysplastic neural tissue in a dural-lined sac filled with CSF through a localized posterior spina bifida. The most common site is the lumbosacral region.

● Source Content Myelomeningocele — Clinical Picture
  • The protruding sac is usually covered with a translucent membrane of modified meninges and skin called the placode.
  • The lesion is soft, but the fluid usually cannot be compressed easily from the sac into the spinal canal because of the small communication with the subarachnoid space.
  • Transillumination produces poor glow.
  • Head circumference may be enlarged due to associated hydrocephalus.
  • Neurological examination shows variable degrees of motor weakness, sensory deficits and sphincteric disturbances.
● Source Content Myelomeningocele — Investigations, D.D. & Treatment

Investigations

Prenatal: fetal ultrasound would show the lesion.

Postnatal:

  • Whole spine MRI: shows the sac, its contents, and may show associated spinal anomalies and congenital syringomyelia.
  • Brain CT: may show associated hydrocephalus.

Differential diagnosis

  • Meningocele
  • Lipomeningomyelocele
  • Lumbosacral lipoma
  • Sacrococcygeal teratoma

Treatment

  • Prevention through dietary supplementation with folic acid.
  • Excision of the sac, untethering of the cord and repair of the dysraphic defects.
● Source Content Meningocele — Definition & Clinical Picture

This is herniation of a sac of dura and arachnoid filled with CSF through a localized posterior spina bifida. The most common site is the lumbosacral region.

  • Protruding sac usually covered with normal skin.
  • Lesion is soft and fluid is compressed easily from the sac into the spinal canal.
  • Transillumination produces a brilliant glow.
  • Head circumference is usually normal and the anterior fontanelle is lax because active hydrocephalus is rare in the immediate neonatal period.
  • Neurological examination is usually normal.

Diagnosis & treatment: similar to meningomyelocele.

● Source Content Other Types of Spina Bifida — Rachischisis

Rachischisis: midline dorsal defect with neural tissue exposure without any sac. Meninges are absent and CSF leaks.

● Source Content Other Types of Spina Bifida — Spina Bifida Occulta

Spina bifida occulta (asymptomatic):

  • Intact overlying skin, with skin stigmata such as tuft of hair or blue skin nevus.
  • A bony defect in the lamina is frequently seen on X-ray and is of no significance.
  • May be discovered accidentally, or may be associated with nocturnal enuresis.
  • The skin may show tuft of hair or nevus.

Treatment: observation.

● Source Content Diastematomyelia

It consists of duplication of the spinal cord (cord splitting), separated by a bony spur which can stretch neural structures.

Management: excision of the bony spur.

● Source Content Hypertrophic Filum Terminale (Tethered Cord)

The cord is below the normal level (L1–L2 disc).

Symptoms

  • Sphincteric disturbances
  • Progressive motor deficit
  • Sensory deficit
  • Back pain
  • Progressive scoliosis

Management: division of the hypertrophied filum terminale.

● Source Content Encephalocele

This is a neural tube defect characterized by cystic malformation in which CNS structures herniate through a cranial defect. It is either cranial meningocele or meningoencephalocele.

● Source Content Subdural Hematoma in Infancy
  • Birth trauma is a frequent cause.
  • Occurs in 10% of battered children; violent shaking of an infant may be sufficient to lacerate bridging veins.
  • Bilateral in 85% of cases and usually over the frontoparietal area.
  • Usually presents with large head size because sutures are unfused, plus non-specific symptoms such as irritability and failure to thrive.
  • Diagnosis: brain CT.
  • Treatment in the source: aspiration, possibly through the open fontanelle and possibly repeated, or application of a subduro-peritoneal shunt.

High-Yield Summary

Concise review for limited study time; the Complete Theory tab remains the full source.

1) Craniosynostosis — Definition & Rule

Premature fusion of one or more cranial sutures. Growth is restricted perpendicular to the fused suture and compensates parallel to it.

2) Craniosynostosis Types

  • Metopic → Trigonocephaly
  • Sagittal (most common) → Scaphocephaly
  • Unicoronal → Anterior Plagiocephaly + harlequin eye
  • Bicoronal → Brachycephaly; may occur with Apert/Crouzon
  • Lambdoid → Posterior Plagiocephaly
  • Pansynostosis → Oxycephaly + marked ↑ intracranial tension

3) Craniosynostosis — Clinical / Investigations / Treatment

Cosmetic disfigurement; neurological problems especially with ≥2 sutures. Restrained brain growth may cause mental retardation, motor impairment, optic atrophy. Raised ICP mostly in pansynostosis.

Plain skull X-ray → fusion/deformity; 3-D CT → thumbprint appearance; CT/MRI for coronal, metopic, lambdoid synostosis. Treatment: suturectomy, cranial remodeling, facial advancement for proptosis.

4) Myelomeningocele

Most common open spinal dysraphism; neural tissue + CSF in dural-lined sac; usually lumbosacral. Placode, poor transillumination, partial/non-easy compression, associated hydrocephalus and neuro deficits.

Prenatal US; postnatal whole-spine MRI; brain CT for hydrocephalus. Prevention: folic acid. Treatment: sac excision + untethering + dysraphic defect repair.

5) Meningocele

CSF-filled dura/arachnoid sac through posterior spina bifida, usually lumbosacral. Normal skin, soft, easily/totally compressible, brilliant/total transillumination, normal head circumference and usually normal neurological examination.

6) Myelomeningocele vs Meningocele

7) Other Spina Bifida / Cord Lesions

  • Rachischisis: exposed neural tissue, no sac, absent meninges, CSF leak.
  • Spina bifida occulta: intact skin ± hair tuft/blue nevus; lamina defect; often incidental; observation.
  • Diastematomyelia: split cord + bony spur → excise spur.
  • Tethered cord: cord below L1–L2 disc; sphincteric disturbance, progressive motor/sensory deficit, back pain, scoliosis → divide hypertrophied filum.

8) Encephalocele & Infantile Subdural Hematoma

Encephalocele: CNS structures herniate through cranial defect; cranial meningocele or meningoencephalocele.

Subdural hematoma in infancy: birth trauma common; 10% battered children; violent shaking may tear bridging veins; bilateral in 85%, usually frontoparietal; large head + irritability/failure to thrive; CT diagnosis.
