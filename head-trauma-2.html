Source Content Brain Concussion

Head injury with temporary loss of brain function. May be caused by a blow to the head or by acceleration-deceleration forces without direct impact.

Clinical picture: instant loss of awareness or alertness for a few minutes up to a few hours. It is the most common type of traumatic brain injury.

Treatment: monitoring and rest. Confusion and amnesia usually subside within three weeks, though they may persist.

Repeated concussions can cause cumulative brain damage such as Dementia pugilistica or severe complications such as Second-impact syndrome.

Source Content Diffuse Axonal Injury (DAI)

DAI refers to extensive lesions in white matter tracts. It is one of the major causes of unconsciousness and persistent vegetative state after head trauma.

  • Occurs in about half of all severe head trauma cases.
  • Also occurs in moderate and mild brain injury.
  • Best diagnosed by MRI brain.

Treatment: no specific treatment beyond general head injury management, including stabilization and limiting rises in ICP.

Source Content Brain Contusion

Brain contusion is a bruise of brain tissue and occurs in 20–30% of severe TBI.

Cerebral contusion is associated with multiple microhemorrhages and small blood-vessel leaks into brain tissue with an intact pia-arachnoid membrane. Cerebral laceration is similar except the pia-arachnoid membranes are torn.

Clinical picture: minor TBI may cause headache, confusion, sleepiness, dizziness, nausea/vomiting, seizures, and difficulty with coordination and movement. Severe TBI may present with loss of consciousness. Signs depend on location; associated edema may cause increased ICP.

Treatment: general management of head trauma; decompressive craniotomy (craniectomy) and duroplasty if midline shift.

Source Content Brain Edema

Serious complication of TBI.

  • Immediate (early) swelling: within minutes of injury.
  • Delayed (late) swelling: peaks at 3–5 days post-injury.

Clinical picture: manifestations of increased ICP and brain herniation in severe cases.

Treatment: general management of head trauma + decompressive craniotomy (craniectomy) and duroplasty.

Source Content Epidural Hematoma (EDH)
  • Common in younger age group because dura strips more readily off underlying bone.
  • Associated with fissure fracture in 90%.

Bleeding source: arterial — middle meningeal artery; venous — middle meningeal vein, venous sinuses, diploic veins.

Site: 75% temporal area; 25% frontal, parietal, occipital and posterior fossa.

Slow EDH: concussion → lucid interval → compression. Rapid EDH: presents directly in compression stage.

Compression: increased ICP (headache, vomiting), deteriorating consciousness, focal neurological signs (dilated pupil, hemiparesis), vital-sign changes.

Uncal lateralization: ipsilateral fixed dilated pupil from oculomotor compression, ipsilateral conjugate eye deviation, contralateral hemiparesis, contralateral exaggerated reflexes/Babinski. Ipsilateral weakness is a false localizing sign — Kernohan's sign.

CT: hyperdense biconvex hematoma with compression of underlying brain and distortion of lateral ventricle.

Treatment: EDH is a surgical emergency. General management + urgent craniotomy and evacuation; stop bleeding with bipolar coagulation/bone wax/gelfoam, dural hitches, drain.

Source Content Subdural Hematoma (SDH)

Acute SDH

<3 days. Usually with severe head injury. Commonly from cortical laceration (secondary SDH) or focal tear of a cortical artery (primary SDH). Neurology may fail to improve or deteriorate. Over 80% have cranial vault or skull-base fracture. CT: hyperdense hematoma, concave toward brain. Treatment: craniotomy or craniectomy and surgical evacuation.

Subacute SDH

4–21 days. CT: isodense hematoma.

Chronic SDH

>21 days. Usually in old patients with atrophic brain and often on anticoagulants. Trauma may be trivial or absent in history. Most common cause: bridging-vein bleeding (primary SDH). Presentation: focal neurological deficit or progressive dementia. Bilateral in 25%. CT: hypodense.

Treatment: burr-hole evacuation + drain + postoperative adequate hydration. Mild hematoma with intact patient may be managed conservatively.

Source Content Intracerebral Hematoma
  • Occurs after severe head injury, penetrating (missile) injury, or depressed skull fracture.
  • Presentation depends on site and size.
  • Brain CT shows size and position.
  • Surgical treatment depends on site, size and clinical presentation.

Surgical indications: large superficial hematoma with mass effect and increased ICP; hematoma in posterior fossa because it is a very tight space.

Source Content Subarachnoid Hemorrhage

Subarachnoid hemorrhage: conservative treatment. Some cases develop post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus later on.

High-Yield Summary

Concussion

Temporary loss of brain function; most common TBI; monitoring + rest; confusion/amnesia usually improve within 3 weeks. Repeated concussion may cause Dementia pugilistica or Second-impact syndrome.

DAI

Extensive white-matter tract injury; major cause of unconsciousness and persistent vegetative state; occurs in about half of severe head trauma and also in mild/moderate injury; MRI best; no specific treatment beyond stabilization and ICP control.

Contusion

Brain bruise, 20–30% of severe TBI; microhemorrhages with intact pia-arachnoid. Laceration = torn pia-arachnoid. Symptoms range from headache/confusion to loss of consciousness; edema may cause raised ICP. Decompression if midline shift.

Brain Edema

Early swelling within minutes; delayed swelling peaks at 3–5 days. Severe cases may cause raised ICP/herniation. General management + decompressive craniectomy/duroplasty.

EDH

Younger age; fissure fracture in 90%; middle meningeal artery important source; 75% temporal. Slow EDH: concussion → lucid interval → compression. CT = hyperdense biconvex. Surgical emergency requiring urgent evacuation.

Uncal Lateralization

Ipsilateral fixed dilated pupil, ipsilateral conjugate gaze deviation, contralateral hemiparesis/reflex exaggeration/Babinski. Ipsilateral weakness = Kernohan's false localizing sign.

SDH

Acute: <3 d, hyperdense concave, >80% skull fracture. Subacute: 4–21 d, isodense. Chronic: >21 d, elderly/atrophic brain/anticoagulant, bridging vein, 25% bilateral, hypodense; burr-hole evacuation + drain + hydration or conservative if mild/intact.

Intracerebral Hematoma

After severe or penetrating injury/depressed fracture. CT defines size/site. Surgery for large superficial hematoma with mass effect/raised ICP or posterior fossa hematoma.

SAH

Conservative treatment; some cases later develop post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus.
