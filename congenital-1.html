● Source Content Hydrocephalus — Definition

It is a disorder of CSF circulation characterized by abnormal increase in CSF volume within the brain ventricles with or without associated increase in intracranial pressure.

● Source Content CSF Circulation

CSF is produced by filtration from the choroid plexus in the lateral and fourth ventricles.

  1. From the lateral ventricles through the foramina of Monroe to the third ventricle.
  2. Through the aqueduct of Sylvius to the fourth ventricle.
  3. The main volume passes through the central foramen of Magendi and lateral foramina of Luschka to the cranial subarachnoid space.
  4. It is absorbed through the arachnoid villi and granulations into the superior sagittal sinus.
  5. A small volume exits the fourth ventricle to the central canal of the spinal cord.
● Source Content Causes

1) Congenital causes

  1. Genetic-induced hydrocephalus “Bicker's-Adams syndrome”
  2. In utero infections
  3. In utero ischemic events
  4. Aqueductal stenosis

2) Acquired causes

  1. Post-meningitic
  2. Intracerebral hemorrhage
  3. Brain tumors
● Source Content Pathogenesis

1) Obstruction

  • Mass obstruction: block of ventricles by tumor or hemorrhage, associated with dilatation of the ventricles proximal to the site of obstruction.
  • Flow obstruction: increased CSF viscosity mainly due to increased proteins as in meningitis or encephalitis.

2) Malabsorption

Superior sagittal sinus venous thrombosis or obstruction.

3) Hypersecretion

Choroid plexus hyperplasia or papilloma.

● Source Content Types

According to the underlying cause and mechanism of ventricular dilatation, hydrocephalus is classified into 2 types:

1. Obstructive

Occurs due to obstruction of the CSF pathway.

  • Non-communicating: no communication between the cerebral ventricles. Occurs in:
    • Aqueductal stenosis
    • Brain tumors
    • Intracerebellar hemorrhage
  • Communicating: communication between the cerebral ventricles; obstruction is at the subarachnoid space. Occurs in:
    • Post meningitis
    • Post intraventricular or subarachnoid hemorrhage

2. Non-obstructive (communicating)

  • Agenesis or hypoplasia of arachnoid villi and granulations
  • Choroid plexus hyperplasia or papilloma or carcinoma
  • Other secretory tumors like schwannoma
● Source Content Clinical Picture — Infantile Hydrocephalus

This type occurs in infants before closure of the anterior fontanelle. It is manifested by:

  1. Poor suckling & feeding and high-pitched cry.
  2. Macrocephaly: abnormal progressive enlargement of the head manifested by abnormal increase in head circumference more than the normal percentile for age. It is bilateral, symmetrical and globular with cephalo-facial disproportion.
  3. Separation of skull sutures.
  4. Wide, tense, bulging fontanelles, especially the anterior fontanelle.
  5. Dilated scalp veins.
  6. Parinaud's sign: loss of upward gaze, progressing to downward deviation of the eyeballs with a rim of sclera above the cornea — “sun-set appearance”. This is due to pressure of the floor of the dilated third ventricle on the tectum of midbrain.
  7. Cranial nerve palsies, especially the VI nerve.
  8. Seizures.
  9. Delayed developmental milestones.
● Source Content Clinical Picture — Adult Hydrocephalus

This type occurs in children and adults after closure of the anterior fontanelle. It is manifested by symptoms and signs of increased intracranial pressure:

  1. Headache, especially morning headache
  2. Vomiting
  3. Blurred vision
  4. Bilateral papilledema
  5. Diplopia and squint due to sixth nerve palsy
  6. Impaired consciousness
● Source Content Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus (Hakim Adam Syndrome)

Dilated ventricular system with normal intracranial pressure.

This type occurs in elderly patients. It is manifested by the triad of:

  1. Dementia (memory loss)
  2. Gait disturbance
  3. Urinary incontinence
● Source Content Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension (Pseudotumor Cerebri)

Manifestations of increased intracranial pressure without dilated ventricles. Lumbar puncture shows increased pressure above normal, and fundus shows papilledema.

● Source Content D.D. of Enlarged Head “Macrocrania” (Infantile Type)
  1. Familial
  2. Hydrocephalus
  3. Bone disease, e.g. Rickets
  4. Arachnoid cyst
  5. Infantile chronic subdural hematoma (shaken baby syndrome i.e. excessive head movements)
● Source Content Treatment — Aim
  1. Treatment of the cause
  2. CSF diversion i.e. shunting
● Source Content Treatment — Definitive Procedures

1. Craniotomy and elimination of the intracranial obstructing lesion

  • Excision of space occupying lesion ex. brain tumor, arachnoid cyst
  • Evacuation of intracranial hematoma

2. Endoscopic removal of intraventricular tumors and cysts

3. CSF diversion procedures

1. Intracranial shunts

  • Endoscopic Third Ventriculostomy: shunting CSF from the third ventricle to the prepontine cistern. It is done endoscopically. Good for posterior fossa tumors i.e. 4th ventricle or aqueduct compression or occlusion.

2. Placement of Intracranial-Extracranial shunt device

  • Ventriculoperitoneal: shunting of CSF from the lateral ventricle to the peritoneal cavity (most commonly used).
  • Ventriculoatrial: shunting of CSF from the lateral ventricle to the right atrium (most physiological).

3. Extracranial shunts

  • Lumboperitoneal shunt: shunting of CSF from the lumbar cistern to the peritoneal cavity.
● Source Content Complications of Shunt Devices
  1. Infection
    • Ventriculitis
    • Peritonitis
    • Endocarditis
  2. Obstruction (valve is sunken or non-compressible): shunt survey is needed to determine site of obstruction.
  3. Disconnection
    • Migration
    • Intestinal perforation (can be seen perianal)
  4. Over-drainage
    • Slit-ventricle
    • Subdural hematoma
    • Craniostenosis
● Source Content Craniostenosis — Definition

It is the premature fusion of one or more cranial sutures separating the membranous bones of the skull. In this condition, one or more of the fibrous sutures in an infant skull prematurely fuses by ossification, thereby changing the growth pattern of the skull. Because the skull cannot expand perpendicular to the fused suture, it compensates by growing more in the direction parallel to the closed sutures.

● Source Content Craniostenosis — Types
  1. Metopic Synostosis: ridging of the metopic suture with bilateral flattening of the frontal bones and anterior displacement of the coronal suture. The resultant deformity is termed Trigonocephaly.
  2. Sagittal Synostosis (most common): ridging of the fused sagittal suture with frontal & occipital bossing and flattening of parietal bones. The head is elongated antero-posteriorly. The deformity is termed Scaphocephaly.
  3. Unicoronal Synostosis: ridging of half of the coronal suture and flattening of the ipsilateral frontal and parietal bones. The orbit is deformed due to elevation of the sphenoid ridge and is commonly described as the “harlequin eye”. The deformity is termed Anterior Plagiocephaly.
  4. Bicoronal Synostosis: ridging of the coronal suture and flattening of the caudal portion of the frontal bones and supraorbital ridges. The skull is broad and short, with expansion in the lateral directions and bilateral harlequin configuration of the orbits. The deformity is termed Brachycephaly.   5. Lambdoid Synostosis: ridging of the fused lambdoid suture with flattening of the parieto-occipital region ipsilateral to the fused suture. The deformity is termed Posterior Plagiocephaly.

● Source ContentFast Review

High-Yield Summary

A concise source-based review for limited study time. It does not replace the Complete Theory tab.

1) Hydrocephalus — Core Definition & CSF Route

Definition: abnormal increase in CSF volume within the brain ventricles, with or without increased intracranial pressure.

Route: Lateral ventricles → foramina of Monroe → 3rd ventricle → aqueduct of Sylvius → 4th ventricle → Magendi & Luschka → cranial subarachnoid space → arachnoid villi/granulations → superior sagittal sinus.

A small volume passes from the 4th ventricle to the central canal of the spinal cord.

2) Causes & Pathogenesis

Congenital: Bicker's-Adams syndrome, in-utero infections, in-utero ischemic events, aqueductal stenosis.

Acquired: post-meningitic, intracerebral hemorrhage, brain tumors.

Mechanisms:

  • Mass obstruction: tumor or hemorrhage.
  • Flow obstruction: increased CSF viscosity from increased proteins, e.g. meningitis/encephalitis.
  • Malabsorption: superior sagittal sinus venous thrombosis/obstruction.
  • Hypersecretion: choroid plexus hyperplasia or papilloma.

3) Types

Obstructive:

  • Non-communicating: aqueductal stenosis, brain tumors, intracerebellar hemorrhage.
  • Communicating: obstruction at subarachnoid space; post-meningitis, post intraventricular/subarachnoid hemorrhage.

Non-obstructive (communicating): agenesis/hypoplasia of arachnoid villi and granulations; choroid plexus hyperplasia/papilloma/carcinoma; secretory tumors like schwannoma.

4) Infantile Hydrocephalus

Occurs before closure of the anterior fontanelle.

  • Poor suckling/feeding + high-pitched cry.
  • Progressive macrocephaly: bilateral, symmetrical, globular, with cephalo-facial disproportion.
  • Separated sutures; wide tense bulging fontanelles; dilated scalp veins.
  • Parinaud's sign / sun-set appearance: loss of upward gaze then downward deviation; due to pressure of the floor of the dilated 3rd ventricle on the tectum of midbrain.
  • VI nerve palsy, seizures, delayed developmental milestones.

5) Adult Hydrocephalus & Special Syndromes

After fontanelle closure: morning headache, vomiting, blurred vision, bilateral papilledema, diplopia/squint due to VI nerve palsy, impaired consciousness.

NPH (Hakim Adam syndrome): dilated ventricles + normal ICP; elderly; triad = dementia + gait disturbance + urinary incontinence.

IIH / pseudotumor cerebri: raised ICP manifestations without dilated ventricles; LP shows raised pressure; fundus shows papilledema.

6) Investigations

  • Maternal cranial ultrasonography: may show enlarged head and hydrocephalus during fetal development.
  • CT/MRI: demonstrate ventricular dilatation and the cause of obstruction in non-communicating hydrocephalus.
  • Source figures include normal CT, hydrocephalus with subarachnoid hemorrhage, normal MRI, hydrocephalic MRI, and aqueductal stenosis.

7) D.D. of Macrocrania in Infant

  1. Familial
  2. Hydrocephalus
  3. Bone disease, e.g. rickets
  4. Arachnoid cyst
  5. Infantile chronic subdural hematoma (shaken baby syndrome / excessive head movements)

8) Treatment

Aims: treat the cause + CSF diversion.

  • Craniotomy and removal of obstructing lesion: tumor/cyst excision; hematoma evacuation.
  • Endoscopic removal of intraventricular tumors/cysts.
  • ETV: 3rd ventricle → prepontine cistern; useful for posterior fossa tumor / 4th ventricle or aqueduct compression/occlusion.
  • VP shunt: lateral ventricle → peritoneum; most commonly used.
  • VA shunt: lateral ventricle → right atrium; most physiological.
  • LP shunt: lumbar cistern → peritoneum.

9) Shunt Complications

  • Infection: ventriculitis, peritonitis, endocarditis.
  • Obstruction: valve sunken/non-compressible; shunt survey to locate obstruction.
  • Disconnection: migration, intestinal perforation (may be seen perianally).
  • Over-drainage: slit ventricle, subdural hematoma, craniostenosis.

10) Craniostenosis

Definition: premature fusion of one or more cranial sutures. Skull cannot expand perpendicular to the fused suture, so it grows more parallel to it.
